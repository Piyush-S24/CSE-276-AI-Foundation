01 Agents, percepts and rationality

What makes an agent "intelligent"?

An agent is anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts on it through actuators. Nothing here requires silicon — a person, a thermostat and a bacterium are all agents by this definition. What separates a good agent from a bad one is a single idea: rationality.

rational agent — for every percept sequence it has seen, a rational agent selects the action it expects will maximise its performance measure, given the evidence its percepts provide and whatever knowledge it was built with.
Percept

One snapshot of input from the sensors at an instant — a camera frame, a temperature reading, a symptom typed into a form.

Percept sequence

The complete history of everything the agent has ever perceived. An agent's choice may legally depend on all of it, not just "now".

Agent function

The abstract map from every possible percept sequence to an action — an infinite table, in principle.

Agent program

The concrete, runnable implementation of that function — the actual code executing on the actual architecture.

Rational ≠ omniscient. A rational agent isn't required to know the future or read the world perfectly — only to act sensibly on what it has actually perceived. A self-driving car that brakes for a plastic bag it mistook for debris is still rational: braking was the sensible response to what it perceived, even though the belief turned out to be wrong.

This is exactly why PEAS exists: you cannot judge or design a rational agent without first pinning down what "performance" and "environment" even mean for it.

05 When one loop isn't enough

Autonomous and multi-agent systems

An autonomous agent acts for long stretches without a human in the loop, relying on its own percepts rather than being pre-programmed for every case. Put several such agents in one shared environment and two new problems appear that a single agent never faces:

Cooperation

Agents share a goal and actively help each other reach it — three warehouse robots splitting one order between them.

Coordination

Agents may not share a goal, but must still avoid working against each other — two robots not colliding in the same aisle.

Human–AI collaboration is coordination with a human as one of the agents. A diagnosis-support system doesn't act on the patient — it perceives (symptoms, tests), reasons, and hands its recommendation to a clinician who acts. The division of labour is the design.
