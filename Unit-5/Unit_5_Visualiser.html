1Generative AI and how it actually works

A language model does exactly one thing: given the text so far, put a probability on every possible next token. Everything else — essays, code, conversation — is that one operation run in a loop.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Almost every misconception about language models dissolves once you see that they do exactly one thing: assign a probability to every possible next token, then pick one. Essays, code and conversation are all that single operation in a loop.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not have it look the answer up in a database?” That is a different system — a search engine — and it is what RAG bolts on precisely because the model has no such database. The model has weights, not records. “Why not make it always pick the most likely word?” You can, by setting temperature to 0 — and for factual answers you usually should. For anything creative it produces flat, repetitive text.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You assume fluent means correct. The model has no step at which it checks whether what it is about to say is true — plausibility and truth are produced by the same mechanism, which is why a fabricated citation reads exactly like a real one.

Next-token prediction
P(ti | t1, …, ti−1) = softmax(zT),   softmaxj(z) = exp(zj) ∑k exp(zk)
The model outputs a raw score (a logit) per token; softmax turns those into probabilities. The temperature T divides the logits before softmax and so controls how peaked the distribution is.

The sentence so far: “the cat sat on the…”

Five candidate next tokens with their logits. Drag the temperature and watch the distribution change — the logits never move, only how sharply they are read.

temperature T =1.00
What a language model is not
It has no database of facts to look things up in, and no step where it checks whether what it is about to say is true. It is producing the statistically plausible continuation. Most of the time the plausible continuation is also the correct one, because the training text was mostly correct — but those are two different properties, and when they come apart you get the next section.
Worked example — how temperature reshapes the same prediction
After “the cat sat on the…” the model outputs these raw logits: mat 4.2, floor 3.1, chair 2.6, roof 1.4, banana −0.8. Softmax turns them into probabilities after dividing by T.
  1. At T = 1 — exponentiate each logite4.2 = 66.69,  e3.1 = 22.20,  e2.6 = 13.46,  e1.4 = 4.06,  e−0.8 = 0.45
  2. Divide by the total (106.85)mat 62.4%, floor 20.8%, chair 12.6%, roof 3.8%, banana 0.4%
  3. Now set T = 0.2 — every logit is multiplied by 5 firstthe gaps are magnified enormously → mat 99.6%, floor 0.4%, everything else essentially 0
  4. And T = 2.0 — every logit is halvedthe gaps shrink → mat 42.5%, floor 24.5%, chair 19.1%, roof 10.5%, banana 3.5%
  5. Measure the uncertainty at each settingentropy = 0.04 bits at T=0.2,  1.48 at T=1,  1.99 at T=2 (the maximum for 5 options is 2.32)
  6. Note what never changedthe logits — the model's actual beliefs were identical in all three cases
At T = 0.2 the output is effectively deterministic; at T = 2.0 “banana” has a 1-in-29 chance of being emitted. Same model, same prediction, entirely different behaviour — which is why temperature belongs in every bug report.

2Prompt engineering

The model is fixed. The only thing you control is what goes into the context window — so that is where the engineering happens.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

You cannot retrain the model, so the context window is the only thing you control. Everything the model knows about your task, your format and your constraints has to arrive in the prompt — which makes prompt design a real engineering surface, not a knack.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not fine-tune instead?” Fine-tuning needs data, compute and repetition every time the model updates; a prompt change takes seconds. Start with the prompt and escalate only when you can say exactly what it failed at. “Why not just ask clearly once?” For common tasks that is enough — few-shot and chain-of-thought exist for the cases where it is not.

What goes wrong if you skip it

An underspecified prompt is filled in with whatever was most common in training. If you did not state the length, the format, the audience or what it must not do, you have not left those free — you have delegated them.

TechniqueWhat you doWhen it helps
Zero-shotjust state the task clearlythe task is common and well specified
Few-shotgive two to five worked examples firstyou need a specific output format or an unusual convention
Chain of thoughtask it to reason step by step before answeringarithmetic, logic, multi-step problems
Role / system promptset who the model is and what the rules arecontrolling tone, scope and refusals consistently
Structured outputspecify a schema and ask for JSONanything a program will have to parse
Decompositionsplit one hard request into several small oneslong tasks where a single prompt degrades

The same question, four prompts

Be specific about the things you will be annoyed about
Most bad output comes from an underspecified prompt rather than a weak model. If you care about the length, the format, the audience or the things it must not do, say so — the model cannot infer a requirement you did not state, and it will fill the gap with whatever was most common in training.
Worked example — the same question, four ways
Task: classify the sentiment of the review “Does the job, nothing special.” Watch what each technique adds.
  1. Zero-shot — just ask“Classify the sentiment of this review.” → “The sentiment is fairly neutral, perhaps slightly positive.” — correct, but a sentence, not a label, and the next call may phrase it differently
  2. Few-shot — pin the label set and the formatshow three examples first: “Broke in a week” → NEG, “Arrived early” → POS, “Does the job” → NEU  →  output is now exactly NEU
  3. Chain of thought — for a task needing reasoning“A shop sells pens at ₹12. Raj buys 7 and pays with ₹100. Think step by step.” → 7 × 12 = 84; 100 − 84 = 16 → ₹16
  4. Why that helpsthe model must emit the intermediate products, so a slip is visible instead of hidden inside a single guessed answer
  5. Role and structure — for anything a program will parse“You are a careful reviewer. If unsure, say unclear. Reply as JSON: {label, confidence, reason}”
  6. What that last one buysa parseable object and an explicit escape hatch — permitting “unclear” measurably reduces confident guessing
Each technique fixes one specific failure: few-shot fixes format drift, chain-of-thought fixes reasoning errors, a schema fixes parsing, and allowing “unclear” fixes forced answers. Reach for the one that matches your failure.

3Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Instead of hoping the model memorised your documents, fetch the relevant ones at question time and put them in the prompt.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

A model's knowledge is frozen at training time and contains nothing about your company, your documents or last week. RAG fixes that without retraining: fetch the relevant passages at question time and put them in the prompt, so the model answers from them.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not fine-tune on our documents?” Fine-tuning teaches style and format well but is a poor way to install facts — and every document change means retraining. Re-indexing takes minutes. “Why not paste all the documents into the prompt?” Context windows are finite and priced per token; retrieval exists to send only the few passages that matter.

What goes wrong if you skip it

If retrieval returns the wrong passage, the model answers confidently from the wrong passage. Most production RAG failures are retrieval failures wearing a generation costume — which is why the evaluation effort belongs on the retriever.

The pipeline, one stage at a time

step 0 of 0

What RAG fixes

The model can answer from documents it never saw in training; the knowledge is current, because you control the index; the answer can be cited, so a reader can check it; and updating knowledge means re-indexing, not re-training.

What RAG does not fix

If retrieval returns the wrong passage, the model will answer confidently from the wrong passage. Garbage retrieved is garbage generated. Most production RAG failures are retrieval failures, not generation failures — so that is where the evaluation effort belongs.

Two pipelines, not one
chunk → embed → index   |   query → embed → top-k → prompt → generate
The left half runs once, offline, over your corpus. The right half runs per question. Chunk size is the parameter people most often get wrong: too small and a chunk loses its context, too large and the embedding becomes a blur of several topics.
Worked example — tracing one question through the pipeline
A support corpus of five chunks, already embedded. The question is “Can I send this back after three weeks?”
  1. Offline, once — chunk and embed the corpusfive chunks: returns policy, refund window, shipping times, courier tracking, staff holidays → five vectors in an index
  2. At question time — embed the query with the same model“Can I send this back…” → (0.88, 0.20, 0.07)
  3. Score every chunk by cosine similarityrefund window 0.998, returns policy 0.996, courier tracking 0.457, shipping times 0.378, staff holidays 0.150
  4. Take the top-2 and build the promptpaste those two chunks above the question, with “answer only from the text below; if it is not there, say so”
  5. Generate, and citethe answer quotes the 30-day window and names the chunk it came from
  6. Notice what made it workthe query shares no words with “refund window” — keyword search would have missed it entirely; the match was on meaning
Had retrieval returned shipping times instead, the model would have produced a fluent, confident answer about delivery. Retrieval quality is the ceiling on the whole system, and no amount of prompt tuning raises it.

4Hallucination and how to reduce it

A hallucination is output that is fluent, confident and wrong. It is not a bug that can be patched out — it follows from what the model is doing.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Hallucination is not a bug that can be patched out — it follows directly from what the model does. It is trained to produce likely text, and a plausible-looking citation is high-probability output whether or not that paper exists. Understanding that is what tells you which mitigations can work.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just tell it to be accurate?” Because it has no internal fact-checker to switch on — the instruction changes the style of the output more than its truth. “Why not use a bigger model?” Bigger models hallucinate less and more convincingly; scale reduces the rate without changing the mechanism.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You put a model where a wrong answer is both costly and unverifiable. Every technique reduces the rate; none drives it to zero. If nobody downstream can check the answer, no amount of prompt engineering makes that placement safe.

Why it happens

The model is trained to produce likely text, not true text. A plausible-sounding citation is high-probability output whether or not that paper exists.

Why it sounds confident

Fluency and accuracy are produced by the same mechanism, so there is no linguistic signal that distinguishes a correct answer from a fabricated one.

Where it concentrates

Specifics the model has weak coverage of: exact numbers, dates, citations, names, API signatures, and anything after the training cut-off.

Why it gets worse under pressure

Asked a question it cannot answer, a model trained to be helpful produces something. Explicitly permitting “I don't know” measurably reduces fabrication.

Mitigations, weakest to strongest

The one that matters most
Do not put a model where a wrong answer is costly and unverifiable. Every technique above reduces the rate; none drives it to zero. The architectural decision — which tasks get a model at all, and where a human stays in the loop — does more for safety than any amount of prompt tuning.
Worked example — watching the mitigations stack up on one question
Question: “What is our refund window, and which policy says so?” The true answer is 30 days, stated in Policy R-14.
  1. Bare model, T = 1.0“Our refund window is 14 days, per Policy R-22.” — fluent, specific, and both figures invented
  2. Add “if you do not know, say so”“I do not have access to your specific policy.” — no longer wrong, but no longer useful either
  3. Lower the temperature to 0.2removes the sampling of unlikely tokens, so it stops inventing varied wrong answers — it still cannot know the right one
  4. Add retrieval, with the policy text in the prompt“The refund window is 30 days (Policy R-14).” — correct, and attributed
  5. Add programmatic verificationcheck that the quoted policy id appears in the retrieved chunk → a fabricated citation is now caught automatically
  6. What is left for a humanonly the cases where retrieval found nothing — a far smaller queue
Prompt-level fixes moved it from confidently wrong to honestly useless. Only grounding in retrieved text made it right, and only verification against something outside the model made it checkable.

5Fairness, bias and transparency

A model can be accurate, calibrated and still harm one group far more than another. Worse, some fairness definitions are mathematically incompatible, so you have to choose.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

A model can pass every aggregate check and still harm one group far more than another. Worse, the obvious fairness definitions are mathematically incompatible when groups have different base rates — so this is not a bug to fix but a trade-off to choose and defend.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just remove race and gender from the features?” Because the model reconstructs them from proxies — postcode, name, purchase history — so the disparity survives while becoming harder to detect. “Why not equalise everything?” You cannot: equalising precision forces the error rates apart, and equalising error rates forces precision apart.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You check only the aggregate metrics. Accuracy and precision can be identical across two groups while one group's innocent members are flagged at six times the rate — and nothing in a standard evaluation report would show it.

Source of biasHow it gets in
Historicalthe data faithfully records a world that was already unequal
Representationsome groups are thinly represented, so the model fits them worse
Measurementthe label is a proxy — arrested standing in for committed a crime — and the proxy is itself biased
Aggregationone model for groups that genuinely behave differently
Deploymentthe model is used on a population or for a purpose it was never validated for

A calibrated model that is still unfair

Two groups, 100 people each. The model has the same accuracy and the same precision for both — by the usual checks it passes. Look at the error rates.

step 0 of 0
Three metrics you cannot satisfy at once
FPR = FPFP + TN,   FNR = FNFN + TP,   PPV = TPTP + FP
The impossibility result. When two groups have different base rates, no classifier can equalise PPV, FPR and FNR at the same time unless it is perfect. Equalising one forces the others apart. There is no technical fix — you must decide which error matters more, and defend that choice.
Transparency is what makes the choice defensible
Since the trade-off cannot be avoided, what responsible practice requires is that it be visible: document the intended use, the populations tested, the metrics per group and the known failure modes. Model cards and datasheets exist for exactly this — not to make the model fair, but to make its unfairness legible to the people affected by it.
Worked example — a model that is calibrated and still unfair
Two groups of 100. Group H has a base rate of 60% (60 genuinely positive); Group L has 25%. The same model scores both. H: TP=52, FP=7, FN=8, TN=33. L: TP=14, FP=2, FN=11, TN=73.
  1. AccuracyH = 52 + 33100 = 0.850;  L = 14 + 73100 = 0.870  →  near identical
  2. Precision — does a flag mean the same thing for both?H = 5259 = 0.881;  L = 1416 = 0.875  →  the model is calibrated
  3. So far it passes every usual check. Now the false positive rateH = 740 = 0.175;  L = 275 = 0.027
  4. Express that as a ratio0.1750.027 = 6.6× — an innocent person in group H is flagged six times more often
  5. And the false negative rateH = 860 = 0.133;  L = 1125 = 0.440 → a genuinely positive person in L is 3.3× more likely to be missed
  6. Why this cannot be fixed by tuningwith different base rates, equal precision forces unequal error rates — Chouldechova's impossibility result
Both groups are harmed, in opposite ways, by a model that passes accuracy and precision checks. The only honest response is to choose which error matters more, say so publicly, and publish the per-group numbers.

6Responsible AI principles and governance

Principles only matter when they are attached to a decision someone has to make.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Principles only matter when attached to a decision somebody has to make. The useful question is never “is this AI ethical?” in the abstract, but what can go wrong here, to whom, and what have we put in place about it?

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not apply the same controls everywhere?” Because a film recommender and a hiring filter carry utterly different risks, and uniform process means either strangling the harmless or under-governing the dangerous. “Why not leave it to the lawyers?” Because most of the decisions — what to optimise, what to log, where a human intervenes — are made at build time by engineers.

What goes wrong if you skip it

Governance added at the end cannot reach the decisions that mattered. If nothing was logged you cannot audit; if there is no override path a human cannot intervene; if the training data was never documented you cannot answer who is in it.

PrincipleThe question it forcesWhat it looks like in practice
Fairnesswho is harmed, and how much more than others?per-group metrics, not just the aggregate
Transparencycan an affected person find out why?model cards, logged decisions, a stated appeal route
Accountabilitywho is answerable when it goes wrong?a named owner; “the model decided” is not an answer
Privacywhose data is in here and did they agree?minimisation, consent, the right to erasure
Securitywhat happens if someone attacks it?prompt-injection and data-poisoning testing
Safetywhat is the worst plausible outcome?red teaming, staged rollout, a kill switch
Human oversightcan a person intervene in time?a real override, not a button nobody is trained to use

Risk decides how much governance you need

Governance is proportionate, not uniform
A film recommender and a model that screens job applicants do not need the same controls. Regulation such as the EU AI Act is built on exactly this idea — obligations scale with the risk the application poses to people. The practical question is never “is this AI ethical?” in the abstract, but “what can go wrong here, to whom, and what have we put in place about it?”
Worked example — classifying four systems by risk, and what each then owes
The same organisation is deploying four AI systems. Obligations should scale with what can go wrong, and to whom.
  1. A spam filter for internal emailworst case: a message is delayed → minimal risk → no specific obligation beyond normal engineering care
  2. A chatbot on the public websiteworst case: a user mistakes it for a person, or acts on a wrong answer → limited risk → must disclose that it is AI
  3. A CV-screening model for hiringworst case: someone is quietly denied a career on a proxy for ethnicity → high risk
  4. What that fourth one therefore owesper-group metrics (not just aggregate), documented training data, logged decisions, a stated appeal route, a named accountable owner, and a real human override
  5. A system that scores employees on social-media behaviourthis is social scoring → unacceptable → do not build it
  6. The test that separates tier 3 from tier 1can this quietly and irreversibly cost a specific person something that matters?
All four are “AI”. Only one needs the full apparatus, and one should not be built at all. Proportionality is the whole design — treating them alike would be both wasteful and unsafe.
