1Symbolic and statistical AI

For roughly thirty years AI meant writing down what you know as rules. Then it meant letting the machine work it out from examples. Understanding why the first approach stalled is the key to understanding everything that followed.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

This is not history for its own sake. Nearly everything that puzzles people about modern AI — why it is so good at recognising a cat and so bad at arithmetic, why it cannot explain itself, why it needs so much data — follows directly from the fact that the field switched from writing rules to learning from examples. Knowing which approach you are looking at tells you what to expect from it.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just write the rules? It would be exact and we could check it.” For some problems you absolutely should — tax calculation and chess legality are rules, not statistics. But try writing the rules for recognising a cat. Every rule you write (pointed ears, whiskers, fur) has endless exceptions, and the exceptions have exceptions. For perception the rule set never converges, which is precisely where the symbolic era stalled.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You pick the wrong tool and lose months. Using machine learning for something with clear rules means collecting data to learn what you already knew, and getting a worse, unverifiable answer. Using rules for perception means writing them forever and never shipping.

The same problem, both ways

Why symbolic AI stalled, and why it is not dead
Writing rules works until the world provides a case you did not anticipate — and for perception the exceptions are effectively infinite. Nobody can write the rules for recognising a cat. But symbolic methods never went away: planners, constraint solvers, SAT solvers and knowledge graphs are symbolic, and much current work is neuro-symbolic, using a learned model for perception and a symbolic layer for the reasoning that has to be checkable.
Worked example — the same task, attempted both ways
Decide whether an email is spam. A symbolic system is given rules; a statistical one is given labelled examples. Watch what happens when a new trick arrives.
  1. Symbolic: write the ruleIF subject contains “FREE” AND sender not in contacts THEN spam
  2. Test it on “FREE money now”rule fires → spam  ✓ and you can point at the exact rule that decided it
  3. Now the spammer writes “F R E E money now”the substring “FREE” is absent → rule does not fire → delivered  ✗
  4. Patch itadd a rule for spaced letters… then for “FR33”, “F.R.E.E”, “ƒree” — the exceptions never end
  5. Statistical: show it 50 000 labelled emails insteadthe model learns that unusual character spacing itself correlates with spam, across every variant at once → spam  ✓
  6. But now ask whythe answer is a weighted sum over thousands of features — no single rule to point at
The symbolic system is auditable but brittle; the statistical one is robust but opaque. Neither property is optional in a real filter, which is why production systems combine them.

2Machine learning and its three types

What distinguishes them is not the algorithm — it is what feedback the system gets.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Which of the three you are doing determines everything else — what data you must collect, what it will cost, which algorithms are even available, and how you will know whether it worked. It is the first decision on any project, and it is decided by what feedback you can get, not by which algorithm sounds best.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just use supervised learning for everything? It performs best.” It does — when you have labels. The catch is that labels are expensive and often impossible: labelling a million customers by “segment” requires someone to first decide what the segments are, which is the very thing you were trying to find out. And for a drone learning to land, nobody can write down the correct motor command for every situation, so there is nothing to supervise with.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You spend three months and a labelling budget on a problem that needed no labels at all — or, worse, you invent the labels yourself, the model learns your assumptions back, and you mistake that for a discovery.

Supervised

Every example comes with the right answer. The system learns the mapping from input to answer. Spam or not, price of a house, which digit is this.

Unsupervised

No answers at all. The system is asked what structure exists. Customer segments, topics in documents, which transactions look unusual.

Reinforcement

No answers, but a reward after acting. The system learns a policy by trying things. Game playing, robot control, scheduling.

Semi-supervised and self-supervised
Real projects are rarely pure. Semi-supervised learning uses a small labelled set alongside a large unlabelled one. Self-supervised learning invents the labels from the data itself — hide a word and predict it, hide part of an image and reconstruct it. That last idea is how every large language model is trained, and it is why they can use text that nobody ever annotated.
Worked example — reading the task type off the data
Four projects. In each case, decide the learning type from what feedback is available — and note the cost that follows from it.
  1. 10 000 past fraud cases, each confirmed or clearedthe answer is attached to every row → supervised. Cost: the labelling already happened, so it is free here
  2. 2 million customers, nothing annotatedno answer exists, and nobody can define the “correct” segment → unsupervised
  3. A drone that must learn to land on a moving platformno correct motor command is known for each state, but each attempt can be scored → reinforcement
  4. Tomorrow's electricity demandyesterday's demand was recorded, so every past day is a labelled example → supervised regression, not clustering
  5. The trap in case 4it looks like time-series pattern finding, but it has labels — so treating it as unsupervised would discard the very information that makes it easy
  6. The one-line testfor one training example, do I have the answer? Yes → supervised. No, but I can score an action → reinforcement. Neither → unsupervised
Case 4 is the instructive one: having labels and not using them is as much a mistake as needing labels you do not have.

3Introduction to deep learning

A neural network is a stack of simple units. The reason depth matters is that a single layer provably cannot represent some very simple functions.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Depth is not a performance tweak — it changes what the network can represent at all. A single layer can only separate classes with a straight line, and the XOR example below is a four-point problem that it provably cannot solve. One hidden layer fixes it, by building new features and solving the problem in terms of those instead of the raw inputs.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just use a wider single layer, or a simpler model?” Width does not help: no number of neurons in one layer creates a curved boundary, so XOR stays unsolvable however wide you make it. Only depth adds that. But the reverse is also true — deep learning is not the default answer. On tabular data of a few thousand rows, logistic regression or gradient boosting usually beats a neural network, trains in seconds, and can be explained to a stakeholder.

What goes wrong if you skip it

Two symmetric failures. Reach for a single layer or a linear model on a problem that is not linearly separable and it will never work, no matter how long you train it or how much data you add — the limit is mathematical, not practical. Reach for deep learning on a small tabular dataset and you get a slower, less accurate, unexplainable model than the thirty-line alternative.

A single neuron
z = n∑i=1 wixi + b,    a = σ(z) = 11 + e−z
One unit: a weighted sum of its inputs, a bias, and a non-linear squashing function. Everything else is copies of this.

XOR — the function that killed the perceptron

XOR is 1 when exactly one input is 1. No straight line separates the two classes, so no single neuron can compute it — a result that stalled neural network research for years. Add one hidden layer and it becomes easy.

And how the weights get found in the first place

Nobody sets those weights by hand. Gradient descent does it: measure how wrong you are, work out which direction reduces the error, take a small step that way, repeat. Below, a single weight w is learned from one training example.

Why one neuron cannot do it
no w1, w2, b satisfy:  b ≤ 0,  w1 + b > 0,  w2 + b > 0,  w1 + w2 + b ≤ 0
The proof. Adding the two middle inequalities gives w1 + w2 + 2b > 0. Combined with the last one, that forces b > 0, contradicting the first. The system is inconsistent, so no single neuron exists.
What depth actually buys
The hidden layer builds new features — here, an OR-like unit and an AND-like unit — and the output layer solves the problem in terms of those instead of the raw inputs. That is the whole idea of deep learning: each layer re-represents the data so the next layer's job is easier. Enough layers and the features stop being hand-designed altogether.
Worked example — the XOR network, computed input by input
A 2–2–1 network. Hidden units h1 = σ(6x1 + 6x2 − 3) and h2 = σ(6x1 + 6x2 − 9); output y = σ(8h1 − 8h2 − 4).
  1. Input (0, 0)z1 = −3 → h1 = 0.047;  z2 = −9 → h2 = 0.000;  z = −3.62 → y = 0.026 → 0  ✓
  2. Input (0, 1)z1 = +3 → 0.953;  z2 = −3 → 0.047;  z = 8(0.953) − 8(0.047) − 4 = +3.24 → y = 0.962 → 1  ✓
  3. Input (1, 0)identical by symmetry → 0.962 → 1  ✓
  4. Input (1, 1)z1 = +9 → 1.000;  z2 = +3 → 0.953;  z = 8(1.000) − 8(0.953) − 4 = −3.62 → 0.026 → 0  ✓
  5. Now read what the hidden layer learnedh1 fires whenever at least one input is 1 — it is an OR unit. h2 fires only when both are — an AND unit
  6. And what the output does with themy = h1 AND NOT h2 — which is exactly XOR
All four correct. In terms of the two new features the hidden layer invented, the problem became linearly separable — and inventing such features is the entire purpose of depth.

4Types and applications of deep learning

Different data has different structure, and each architecture is a bet about what that structure is.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Each architecture is a built-in assumption about the shape of your data, and a correct assumption is worth an enormous amount of training data. A CNN already “knows” that a pattern means the same thing wherever it appears in an image; a plain network would have to learn that separately for every position, from examples. Choosing the right architecture means choosing what the model gets for free.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not use one general network for everything, since they are universal approximators?” They are — in theory, given unlimited data. In practice a fully connected network on a 224×224 image needs about 50 000 weights per neuron in the first layer alone and must learn translation invariance from scratch. A CNN encodes it structurally and uses a few hundred weights. “Can represent” and “can learn from the data you actually have” are completely different claims.

What goes wrong if you skip it

The model trains, the loss goes down, and it needs ten to a hundred times more data to reach the same accuracy — or never reaches it. Nothing will error. It will simply be quietly, expensively worse than the right architecture would have been.

Why the Transformer displaced the RNN

One architecture, many modalities
The Transformer's assumption — learn which elements relate to which — turns out to be weak enough to fit almost any data you can turn into a sequence of tokens. That generality, more than any single benchmark, is why the same architecture now underpins text, images, audio and protein structure, and why foundation models became possible at all.
Worked example — why a CNN needs so many fewer parameters
A 224×224 RGB image, and a first layer of 64 units. Compare a fully connected layer with a convolutional one.
  1. Count the inputs224 × 224 × 3 = 150 528 numbers per image
  2. Fully connected: each neuron sees every inputone neuron needs 150 528 weights
  3. For 64 such neurons64 × 150 528 = 9 633 792 parameters, in the first layer alone
  4. Convolutional: each filter is a 3×3 patch across 3 channels3 × 3 × 3 = 27 weights + 1 bias = 28 per filter
  5. For 64 filters64 × 28 = 1 792 parameters — for the whole layer
  6. Compute the ratio9 633 7921 792 ≈ 5 376× fewer
And the saving is not merely arithmetic. The dense layer would have to learn separately that a cat in the top-left is still a cat; the CNN gets that for free because the same 28 weights are applied everywhere. Fewer parameters and a better assumption — which is what choosing an architecture buys you.

5Foundation models and modern paradigms

The change was not a better architecture. It was a change in what you train and how often.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Pre-training is enormously expensive and happens once; adapting that model to a new task is cheap and happens constantly. That single shift in where the cost sits is why a student can now build something in an afternoon that would have needed a research team and a labelled dataset ten years ago. It is an economic change, not an algorithmic one.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not train our own model on our own data? It would fit us better.” Occasionally true and usually not worth it: you would need a comparable corpus, comparable compute, and the expertise to use both. Start by prompting an existing model, then add retrieval, then fine-tune only if you can say precisely what the cheaper options failed at. Going straight to training from scratch is the most common way to spend a year and lose.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You rebuild something that already exists, badly. Months of GPU time and data collection to reach a model worse than what a well-written prompt would have given you on day one — and you only discover it at the end, when you finally compare against the baseline you skipped.

The old way and the new way

The economics

Multimodal AI

One model handling text, images and audio together, because they are all encoded as tokens in a shared space. Lets you ask a question about a picture.

Agentic AI

The model does not just answer — it plans, calls tools, observes the result and decides what to do next. The hard parts are reliability over long chains and knowing when to stop.

Edge AI

Inference on the device. Chosen for latency, privacy and working without a network, not for accuracy.

Tiny AI (TinyML)

Models small enough for a microcontroller — kilobytes of memory, running on a battery for months. Keyword spotting and vibration monitoring live here.

Worked example — when does pre-training actually pay off?
Training a model from scratch costs about ₹40 000 of compute and data work per task. Pre-training one foundation model costs ₹10 lakh, after which adapting it to a task costs about ₹500.
  1. The old way, for k taskscost = 40 000 k
  2. The foundation-model waycost = 10 00 000 + 500k
  3. For a single taskscratch ₹40 000 versus foundation ₹10 00 500 → scratch wins by far
  4. For 5 tasks₹2 00 000 versus ₹10 02 500 → scratch still wins
  5. Find the break-even point40 000k = 10 00 000 + 500k → 39 500k = 10 00 000 → k ≈ 25 tasks
  6. For 100 tasks₹40 00 000 versus ₹10 50 000 → foundation wins almost 4-fold
The paradigm is not better algorithms — it is amortisation. Below about 25 tasks it does not pay; above it, it pays enormously. That is why it took over in organisations with many tasks and not in single-purpose projects.

6From cloud to microcontroller

The same task can run in four very different places, and the trade-off is rarely about accuracy.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Some decisions have a deadline measured in milliseconds. A robot deciding whether to brake, a hearing aid suppressing noise, a sensor in a basement with no network — none of these can wait for a round trip to a data centre. Running the model on the device itself is what makes those applications possible at all.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not always use the biggest, most accurate model in the cloud?” Because accuracy is not the binding constraint here — time is. A 98% model that answers in 260 ms is useless for a 20 ms control loop, while a 79% model answering in 4 ms is usable. There are also two constraints accuracy cannot address at all: data that must not leave the device for privacy reasons, and devices with no reliable connection.

What goes wrong if you skip it

The robot hits the obstacle. It correctly identified it — 180 ms after it needed to. This is the case where a worse model is the right engineering decision, and it is genuinely counter-intuitive until you have seen the latency budget written down.

Latency is a correctness requirement, not a nicety
A model that must decide whether to brake cannot wait 200 ms for a network round trip, and a device in a factory basement may have no network at all. In those settings a smaller, less accurate model that answers in 10 ms on the device beats a better one in the cloud — which is why quantisation, pruning and distillation are engineering disciplines in their own right.
Worked example — does the model fit inside the control loop?
A robot moving at 1.5 m/s runs its control loop at 50 Hz and must stop for obstacles. Cloud model: 98% accurate, 260 ms round trip. On-device model: 88% accurate, 18 ms.
  1. Time available per cycle150 s = 20 ms for sense, decide and act together
  2. Cloud model against that budget260 ms → misses 26020 = 13 consecutive cycles
  3. Convert to distance travelled while blind1.5 m/s × 0.260 s = 0.39 m
  4. On-device model18 ms < 20 ms → fits, with 2 ms spare
  5. Its blind distance1.5 × 0.018 = 0.027 m — under 3 cm
  6. Weigh the two failuresthe 88% model mis-classifies 12 obstacles in 100; the 98% model mis-classifies 2 — but is 39 cm late on all of them
39 cm versus 3 cm. The less accurate model is the correct choice, because latency here is a correctness constraint and accuracy is only a quality one. This single calculation is the whole argument for edge and tiny AI.
